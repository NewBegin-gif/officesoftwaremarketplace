We tested it · 2026-09-26

On 26 September 2026 we built the same class-booking web app in Lovable, Replit and Emergent from one identical prompt, each on its free plan, and checked every result against 18 tests we wrote down before the first build. We changed no code: the builder only got our prompt and, where a test failed, a repair prompt in fixed words.

  • Emergent asked five clarifying questions before starting (we let it choose), used a separate design agent and testing agent, and had a working preview in about nine minutes.
  • The first version let ten simultaneous bookings into an eight-spot class, put the admin password in the CSV export link, saved a name made only of spaces and was 494 px wide on a 390 px phone screen.
  • The first repair fixed three of those four. While clearing test data it removed every booking in the app, ours included, and with it the database index its capacity fix relied on, so overbooking still happened. The second repair added a lock and held at eight; it removed all bookings again while cleaning up.

What the pricing page doesn’t say

The catch

Now do the division, because Emergent's own pricing page doesn't. Standard is $20 for 100 credits, so 20 cents a credit. Pro is $200 for 750, which is 26.7 cents. Moving up a tier makes every unit of work more expensive, not less: Pro costs 33% more per credit than Standard, and annual billing keeps the gap (17 cents against 22.3). That is the opposite of the volume discount the ladder looks like -- you are buying a 1M-token context window, custom agents and priority support, not cheaper work.

And there is nowhere cheap to go above it: Business and Enterprise are both quote-only, so passing 750 credits a month means a sales call rather than a bigger plan. Check what your month actually needs before you upgrade; one Standard month plus purchased extra credits may beat one Pro month. AI-generated code still needs a human eye: review it for security, correctness and maintainability before anything touches real users.

When the paid plan is worth it

This ladder runs backwards, and it is worth checking the arithmetic yourself before upgrading. Standard is $20 a month for 100 credits, which is 20 cents a credit. Pro is $200 for 750, which is 26.7 cents -- a third MORE per credit than the tier below it. Annual billing keeps the gap rather than closing it: $17 against $167 works out at 17 cents a credit versus 22.3. So Standard is the cheapest credit on the menu and Pro is the dearest; you are paying a premium to buy in bulk rather than getting a discount for it.

That makes the reason to move up something other than price. Pro adds a 1M-token context window, ultra thinking, system-prompt editing, custom agents and priority support -- capabilities rather than cheaper work, and that is the case you have to make for it, because on credits alone it does not stand up. What upgrading does not solve is the credit ceiling itself. Above Pro there is no published tier to climb to: Business and Enterprise are both quoted, so the moment 750 credits a month is not enough you are in a sales conversation rather than picking a bigger plan.

Extra credits can be bought as needed on Standard and above, which is the real release valve. Count the work you actually plan before choosing a tier.

Try this before you buy

Partially tested · checklist v1.0

Build a class-booking app and try to break it. A one-person yoga studio takes class bookings. Visitors book, move or cancel; only the owner sees and cancels all bookings and exports them.

Anyone deciding between AI app builders for a small booking, sign-up or reservation app: whether the builder handles capacity, double bookings, a protected admin page, export and data that survives republishing.

We ran 16 of 18 checks on Emergent (Free, Web app) on 2026-09-26, with 2 repair rounds. Not run: T16, T18. One run on one day; re-run the checks on the plan you would buy.

  • T01 App published. Loads at a public URL without logging in to the builder. Our run: failed.
  • T05 Full is full. Ten simultaneous booking requests on an empty 8-spot class: 8 accepted, 2 refused. One manual double click is not this test; use simultaneous-bookings.js. Our run: failed in the first version, passed after repair.
  • T11 Password not exposed. The admin password is not in the page source, the JavaScript or any URL. Our run: failed in the first version, passed after repair.
  • T16 Data survives. Bookings are still there after a reload and after republishing. Our run: not tested.
  • T18 Take it with you. Export the code (GitHub or zip) and the data; try to run it outside the builder and note what does not come along. Our run: not tested.

How to actually use Emergent

Start on Standard and measure before you climb, because the ladder works against you. Standard is 20 cents a credit and Pro is 26.7 -- moving up makes every unit of work more expensive rather than less. Spend a month on Standard's 100 credits and record what your actual changes cost: simple edits run 1 to 2 credits, medium features 5 to 10, complex refactors 10 to 20 and beyond.

Then do the division the pricing page does not. If your month needs 150 credits, check whether one Standard month plus purchased extra credits beats one Pro month before you upgrade -- you are buying context window, custom agents and support at the higher tier, not cheaper work. Scope your prompts to the credit bands. A request phrased as one complex refactor costs 10 to 20 credits; the same work split into three medium changes you can check between is 15 to 30 but fails cheaper when it goes wrong.

Early on, while you are learning how this model interprets your codebase, smaller steps waste fewer credits. And review everything before it touches real users. This is an excellent way to get from a prompt to a working prototype or MVP quickly, and it is not a hands-off factory for production software. Read the generated code for security, correctness and maintainability, and budget that reading time as part of the cost.

Frequently asked questions

What should I weigh before buying IT & Productivity software?

Judge it on evidence rather than adjectives. Look for a published security page, single sign-on and role controls on the plan you would actually buy (they are often gated to higher tiers), a status page with real incident history, and a clear answer on where your data is stored. If the tool will hold anything sensitive, those four answers matter more than any feature comparison.
