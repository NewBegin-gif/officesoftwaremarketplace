accessiBe Review (2026)

IT & ProductivityResearched assessment · by Daniel HaketUpdated 2026-09-01

The thing you most need to know about this product is not on its pricing page, it is on the FTC’s.

Best for: small and medium sites on ordinary CMS templates whose owners want a visible, cheap first step — contrast, text sizing, keyboard hints and an accessibility statement — and who understand they are buying an aid rather than conformance. The visit-based tiers are honest for that use: at 5,000 visits a month $490 a year is real money but not a project budget, and the Scale tier is the only one that includes any human testing at all.
Who should skip it: if you need actual WCAG conformance, are responding to a demand letter, or run a complex web app with custom components — in all three the money belongs in an audit and code fixes, and an overlay will not stand in for either. Skip it too if you are shopping on the promise of automatic compliance. That promise is what the FTC ordered the company to stop making without evidence, so treat any version of it you still see as a reason to look harder, not to buy.

What the pricing page doesn’t say

The catch

In January 2025 the Federal Trade Commission ordered accessiBe to pay $1 million to settle allegations that it misrepresented what accessWidget does; the Commission approved the order as final in April 2025. The FTC’s position was that accessiBe’s claim that its AI plug-in could make any website conform to WCAG was false, misleading or unsubstantiated — the widget did not make all customer sites WCAG-compliant. The order now bars the company from claiming its automated products make a site WCAG-compliant, or keep it compliant over time, unless it holds evidence to back that up.

It also requires disclosing the domain limitations of those products before you incur a financial obligation. There is a second finding that belongs on a page like this one. The FTC also alleged accessiBe formatted third-party articles and reviews so they read as independent opinion while failing to disclose the company’s material connections to those reviewers; the order requires it to disclose such connections from here on. We are an affiliate of accessiBe and we get paid if you sign up through our link — which is exactly the sort of connection that case was about, and exactly why it is stated here rather than in a footnote.

What that means in practice: price this as a partial aid, not as legal cover. An overlay sits on top of a site and adjusts it in the browser. It cannot rewrite bad markup, fix an inaccessible custom component or make a keyboard trap go away, and no vendor’s automated layer can promise the conformance a real audit and remediation deliver. If you are buying to reduce the chance of a demand letter, buy the audit — the widget is not that.

And the structural point behind the FTC order: accessWidget adjusts the page in the browser at load time rather than fixing the underlying markup, so the remediation exists only while the widget is being served. Stop paying and the site is exactly as accessible as it was before you started — which is the difference between renting a compliance layer and fixing a site.

What accessiBe does

Web-accessibility widget (accessWidget) that scans a site every 24 hours and adjusts it in the browser — contrast, text sizing, keyboard hints — plus an accessibility statement. Priced by monthly visits: $490/year up to 5,000, $1,490 up to 30,000, $3,990 up to 100,000. An aid, not WCAG conformance — for that the same vendor sells accessFlow (developer audits with ready-to-commit code fixes) and accessServices (VPATs, file remediation, expert audits), both quoted rather than priced publicly.

What buyers report about accessiBe

Recurring themes from the buyer-review sites listed at the foot of this page, read in August 2026. We have not run accessiBe ourselves, so this is other buyers' experience rather than ours. We summarise it here because the complaints are usually the part a vendor page leaves out.

What they praise
  • • Installation is genuinely one line and reviewers keep saying so — a trial installed on WordPress in about a minute and worked immediately, and the widget can be styled to match the site rather than sitting on top of it (Capterra, October 2021).
  • • Live support is rated quick and knowledgeable by a retail e-commerce director who had worked through several other ADA compliance products first (Capterra, April 2025).
  • • Site owners without a development budget value what it replaces: a speech-language pathologist building sites for other practitioners describes it as achieving in a tool what would otherwise be backend work (Trustpilot, January 2023).
What they complain about
  • • Cancellation and auto-renewal dominate the recent record. Reviewers describe a 30-day cancellation window they say was introduced without their agreement (June 2026), a cancellation sent on the stated deadline date but refused because it arrived after 5pm Eastern (August 2026), and a renewal charge pursued weeks after cancelling (August 2026).
  • • Agency billing goes wrong in ways that are hard to unwind: a partner reports a client licence being moved to the client's own account while the payment method stayed attached to the agency, so the agency's card was charged months later for a licence it did not hold (August 2026).
  • • Compliance claims are questioned by reviewers who tested them. A free-trial reviewer writes that despite claims on the website and in a sales call the product will not guarantee full WCAG 2.1 AA compliance (Capterra, April 2023), and another found their site passing accessiBe's own audit tool while other audit tools reported failures.
  • • Pricing transparency and surprise charges: a reviewer was billed $389 for renewal of a licence on a site that no longer exists (Capterra, March 2026), and another calls the pricing untransparent and the subscription difficult to cancel (Capterra, December 2024).

Where the ratings disagree. The two platforms are measuring different moments. Capterra sits at 4.6 from 53 reviews and is dominated by installation and day-to-day use; Trustpilot sits at 2.5 from 248 and is dominated by what happens at renewal — 86% of those reviews are five stars, but the score is time-weighted and the recent fifteen are mostly about cancellation. Trustpilot also flags on the profile that the company has received regulatory attention. The practical reading: the widget installs as advertised and the disputes are about contract terms and compliance claims, so settle the cancellation notice period and what compliance is actually guaranteed, in writing, before the first renewal.

When the paid plan is worth it

The ladder is priced on monthly website visits and billed yearly: Micro $490 a year up to 5,000 visits, Growth $1,490 up to 30,000, Scale $3,990 up to 100,000. Per thousand monthly visits that works out at about $98 a year on Micro, $50 on Growth and $40 on Scale, so the rate improves as you climb -- but the steps are large in absolute terms and the ceilings are what force the move.

The more useful way to read the upgrades is that you are buying legal cover, not software. Micro is the automated product: scans and fixes every 24 hours, an accessibility statement and report, email and chat. Growth adds a dedicated case manager, Google Analytics integration, multi-account management and a two-day response target. Scale is where the human expertise starts -- one hour with an ADA attorney, a yearly round of manual testing and validation by their experts, and custom fixes.

Enterprise adds 5 to 10 attorney hours, on-demand testing and SSO. If your reason for buying is exposure to a demand letter rather than compliance in principle, the automated tiers are not the ones that answer it. Two gaps to plan around. Monthly billing exists but the page prints no monthly figures at all -- only that paying yearly saves 30% -- so you cannot work out the monthly cost from the page and have to ask.

And there is no free plan and no trial listed, so the first commitment is a full year.

How to actually use accessiBe

Installation is per licence rather than per account. Log in, open My Licenses, select the site, then Install and customize widget, and either Copy to Clipboard or Send to developer — accessiBe's own two options — and paste the snippet inside your builder's footer menu or just before the closing body tag. Dedicated guides cover WordPress, Shopify, Wix, Squarespace and Webflow among others, if you would rather not touch code, and their help centre puts a free 7-day trial on each newly registered site.

What to expect afterwards is worth setting straight. accessiBe says accessWidget improves accessibility and usability in as little as 48 hours, then rescans and remediates every 24 hours. Before you assume coverage, read the excluded-issues article their pricing page links to: it lists what the widget does not remediate. Plans are sold on monthly website visits, so have that number to hand — the tier you need is decided by traffic, not by the size of the site.

Distilled from accessiBe's help centre article 'How to install accessWidget on your website' at support.accessibe.com, plus the accessWidget product page and the accessWidget pricing page at accessibe.com, all read 20-21 August 2026.

The natural comparison is UserWay or a manual WCAG audit — the two honest comparisons, and they are not the same choice. UserWay is the other large overlay vendor and the like-for-like alternative; we have not read its price list ourselves, so verify it rather than taking a number from a comparison site — including ours. The real comparison, though, is against paying an accessibility specialist to audit and fix the site. That costs more up front and produces no widget, but it is the only route that changes what a screen-reader user actually encounters and the only one that stands up if conformance is ever tested. Worth knowing before you look elsewhere for it: accessiBe sells that work itself, under two names the widget tiers above do not cover. accessFlow is the developer-side product — it crawls pages and user flows, hands back prioritised issues with code examples and auto-resolves the ones it can, with an IDE integration and an SDK for catching issues before production. accessServices is the human side: VPATs, PDF and document remediation, litigation support, expert audits and manual testing by testers with disabilities. Neither prints a price; both pages route you to a product tour or an expert call, so treat that cost as unknown until you have it in writing.
